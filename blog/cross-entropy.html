Cross-Entropy: From Boltzmann to Backprop

If you have trained a classifier, you have almost certainly minimized cross-entropy. Here is the whole idea in one sitting: each section gives an everyday analogy (the green boxes), the key math, and a figure you can play with. Panels marked ▸ hold the proofs, extra losses, and code, so you can open them only when you want more.

The whole post in five lines
  • Entropy measures randomness: the log of the effective number of equally likely outcomes. An outcome of probability p carries −log p bits of information.
  • Cross-entropy H(p, q) is the average information you need when reality follows p but you model it with q. It never drops below H(p); the extra is the KL divergence.
  • In physics these are Gibbs entropy, the Boltzmann distribution, and free energy. The softmax is a Boltzmann distribution.
  • Minimizing cross-entropy is maximum likelihood, and for a softmax the gradient is simply q − p: belief minus truth.
  • Log loss, BCE, KL, distillation, focal loss, contrastive losses, and perplexity are all cross-entropy with a twist.

1. Randomness, information, and entropy

In plain English

Randomness is about how many possibilities there are. A coin has 2 equally likely outcomes, a die has 6, a shuffled deck about 1068. Entropy is the logarithm of that count: a coin is 1 bit (one yes/no question settles it), a die is log2 6 = 2.58 bits. When outcomes are unequal, entropy counts the effective number of possibilities: a die that lands on 6 ninety percent of the time behaves like a die with only about 1.6 sides.

Start with one outcome. A good measure of the information it carries should be large for rare outcomes, zero for certain ones, and additive for independent ones. The only function that does this is the logarithm: an outcome of probability p carries

I(x) = −log p(x)

With log base 2 the unit is the bit; with the natural log it is the nat (1 nat ≈ 1.443 bits), which is what ML libraries use. Entropy is the average information over all outcomes:

H(p) = −Σx p(x) log p(x)     (and 2H is the effective number of outcomes)

Example. A weather station reports one of four conditions with probabilities 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, 1/8. The ideal code gives each outcome −log2 p bits, so common outcomes get short codes:

OutcomepInformation = ideal code lengthp × length
Sunny0.51 bit0.500
Cloudy0.252 bits0.500
Rain0.1253 bits0.375
Snow0.1253 bits0.375
Entropy H(p), and 2H = 3.36 effective outcomes1.75 bits

A plain 2-bit code uses 2 bits every time; the optimal code averages 1.75. Hold on to that 0.25-bit gap.

2. Where it comes from: physics

In plain English

A brand-new sorted deck of cards has one arrangement: low entropy. A shuffled deck could be in any of 1068, and nothing you can see tells you which: high entropy. Physical entropy is "which arrangement is it, exactly?" that you are missing.

Boltzmann counted arrangements: W equally likely microstates give S = kB ln W. Gibbs generalized it to unequal probabilities, S = −kB Σ pi ln pi, which is Shannon's entropy up to the constant kB and the log base. Among all distributions with a fixed average energy, the one with the highest entropy (the least biased guess) is the Boltzmann distribution:

pi = e−Ei/kBT / Z

That is exactly a softmax over negative energies. The softmax in your network is a Boltzmann distribution, with logits as −E/kBT and a temperature that works the same way (Figure 6, in the extras below, lets you turn it).

In plain English

Equilibrium is the bottom of a bowl. A system that has not settled is a marble partway up the side. The KL divergence to the equilibrium distribution measures how high up the bowl it sits, in units of thermal energy, so it is an excess free energy: KL(q ‖ p) = (F[q] − Feq)/kBT ≥ 0. Minimizing a KL divergence in variational inference is minimizing a free energy.

From W to entropy: counting arrangements, step by step (two figures, and more detail inside)

1. What W is. Many detailed arrangements (microstates) share one big picture (macrostate). W, the multiplicity, counts them. For 4 coins, with "number of heads" as the big picture:

Big pictureArrangementsWChance (W / 16)

2. How W and entropy are related. The entropy of a big picture is log W (Boltzmann's S = k ln W). More arrangements means more ways the system could be, so more uncertainty about which one it is. The log is there because independent systems multiply their counts (6 × 6 = 36) while uncertainty should add (log 36 = log 6 + log 6).

3. From W to −Σ p log p. For N items with n1, n2, … in each category, W = N! / (n1! n2! …). Replacing each ln m! with m ln m − m, then writing ni = N pi, gives

(ln W) / N ≈ −Σ pi ln pi = H

So entropy per item is log W per item, once there are many items. For fractions (0.5, 0.3, 0.2) the formula gives 1.4855 bits per item, and the exact count (log2 W)/N is 1.130 at N = 10, 1.418 at N = 100, 1.475 at N = 1,000, 1.484 at N = 10,000.

Step 3 in detail: the algebra, line by line

Take the log of W: ln W = ln N! − ln n1! − ln n2! − …

Replace every ln m! with m ln m − m: ln W ≈ (N ln N − N) − Σ (ni ln ni − ni). The −N and the +Σ ni cancel, because the counts add up to N, leaving ln W ≈ N ln N − Σ ni ln ni.

Write each count as a fraction, ni = N pi: Σ ni ln ni = Σ N pi (ln N + ln pi) = N ln N · Σ pi + N Σ pi ln pi = N ln N + N Σ pi ln pi, since the fractions add to 1.

Subtract: ln W ≈ N ln N − N ln N − N Σ pi ln pi = −N Σ pi ln pi. Divide by N.

Step 3 in detail: why ln m! ≈ m ln m − m (with a picture)

A factorial is 1 × 2 × … × m, so ln m! = ln 1 + ln 2 + … + ln m: a staircase of bars. The smooth curve ln x runs through the tops of the bars, so the area under it is nearly the same as the staircase, and that area is m ln m − m + 1.

Step 3 in detail: how good is the approximation? (m = 1 to 15)

4. One row versus the whole system. W belongs to a single row, so on its own it does not describe the system. The whole picture has two layers: which row the system is in (chance W / total), and which arrangement inside the row (log W bits of uncertainty). With many items, almost everything sits in the rows near the busiest one, so that row's log W per item is the whole system's.

The two quantities take different inputs. log W uses the fractions seen in a chosen row. −Σ p log p uses the source's true probabilities, so it already describes the whole system. They give the same number per item for the row that matches the true probabilities, which is the row the system actually sits in.

The gap of about 5 bits is the uncertainty about which row (it grows like ½ log2 N, so per flip it shrinks to zero). The 0.75 coin shows why the entropy is not "log of the total number of sequences" (400 bits): its sequences are not equally likely, so only the typical ones, in the row that matches the coin, count.

A wrong row. For a fair coin, the row with 100 heads in 400 flips has its own entropy (0.80 bits per flip), but its chance is W / 2400, and log2 of that is 320.1 − 400 = −79.9, so about 2−80, roughly one in a trillion trillion. A fair coin never lands there, so that row says nothing about it. If you did see 25% heads, you would conclude the coin is biased.

Step 4 in detail: how to get W from observed data (300 heads in 400 flips)

You need only N and the observed counts. W = 400! / (300! · 100!). In logs, log2 400! = 2886.1, log2 300! = 2041.3, log2 100! = 524.8, so log2 W = 2886.1 − 2041.3 − 524.8 = 320.1 bits, or about 1096 sequences. Per flip that is 0.800, against the formula's 0.811 for fractions (0.75, 0.25). This assumes the flips are independent heads-or-tails; in physics W comes from a model of the possible microscopic configurations.

5. Reading −Σ p log p. p is how often an outcome happens. −log p is the information it carries, in bits (log p itself is negative). p · (−log p) weights that information by how often it occurs, and Σ adds them up: entropy is the weighted average information per outcome. The weather example: 0.5·1 + 0.25·2 + 0.125·3 + 0.125·3 = 1.75 bits.

Step 5 in detail: why does a rare outcome carry a lot of information?

Because it narrows things down a lot. Five ways to see it:

  • 20 Questions. One of 2 things takes 1 question, one of 8 takes 3, one of 1,000 about 10, one of 1,000,000 about 20. A rare outcome is one among many, so naming it takes many questions' worth of information.
  • News. "The sun rose" tells you nothing new; "a meteor hit the town" changes your picture of the day.
  • Belief update. You gave it probability p and it happened, so your belief rose by a factor 1/p. Information is the log of that factor, −log p. A 1-in-1,000 event multiplies your belief 1,000 times, about 10 doublings, so 10 bits.
  • Code length. Frequent events get short codes and rare ones long codes: in Morse, E is one dot and Q is a long string.
  • Additivity. Two independent 1-in-1,000 events together are a 1-in-1,000,000 event, and 10 bits + 10 bits = 20 bits, which only the log gives.

A rare event carries many bits each time it happens, but it seldom happens, so in the average its large information gets a tiny weight. Contribution p · (−log2 p): 0.066 at p = 0.01, 0.332 at 0.1, 0.5 at 0.25, 0.5 at 0.5, 0.137 at 0.9. The biggest share, 0.53, is at p ≈ 0.37.

3. Cross-entropy: modeling randomness with the wrong distribution

In plain English

Morse code gave short codes to common English letters (E is one dot). Use that codebook for Finnish, whose letters are used at different rates, and every message comes out longer than necessary. Cross-entropy is the average message length with the wrong codebook; entropy is the length with the perfect one; their difference is the KL divergence.

You build a code from beliefs q, but outcomes arrive at the true rate p. Each outcome costs −log q(x) bits, so the average cost is

H(p, q) = −Σx p(x) log q(x) = H(p) + KL(p ‖ q),   KL(p ‖ q) = Σ p log(p/q) ≥ 0KL is zero only when q = p

Numbers. For the weather station, p = (½, ¼, ⅛, ⅛) with H(p) = 1.75 bits:

Belief qH(p, q)KL(p ‖ q)KL(q ‖ p)
(½, ¼, ⅛, ⅛), the truth1.75000
(¼, ¼, ¼, ¼), uniform2.0000.2500.250
(0.7, 0.1, 0.1, 0.1)1.9180.1680.143

Believing uniform costs the 0.25-bit gap from Section 1. Note the last row: KL(p ‖ q) ≠ KL(q ‖ p), so KL is a divergence, not a distance. Try it with a coin:

Proofs: KL ≥ 0, the free-energy identity, and Landauer's principle

KL ≥ 0. Since −log is convex, Jensen's inequality gives Σ p·(−log(q/p)) ≥ −log Σ p·(q/p) = −log 1 = 0, with equality only if q/p is constant, which forces q = p.

Free energy. With pi = e−βEi/Z, −ln pi = βEi + ln Z, so H(q, p) = β⟨E⟩q + ln Z. Subtracting H(q) = S[q]/kB gives KL(q ‖ p) = β(⟨E⟩ − TS[q]) + ln Z = β(F[q] − Feq), using Feq = −kBT ln Z. For a two-level system with energies 0 and ε at kBT = ε, Z = 1.368 and the probabilities are 0.731 and 0.269.

Landauer. Erasing one bit must dissipate at least kB T ln 2 of heat (about 3 × 10−21 J at room temperature): information is physical.

4. From coding to learning

In plain English

A quiz show asks you to spread 100% of your confidence over the possible answers, then charges you according to how little you put on the right one: a little at 90%, more at 50%, an enormous amount at 1%. That is the cross-entropy loss. Hedging costs a little; being confidently wrong costs a lot.

A classifier outputs a distribution qθ(y | x). The label is the true distribution p, a one-hot vector, so the loss collapses to the log-probability of the right class:

L(θ) = H(p, qθ) = −Σk pk log qk = −log qθ(y | x)

This is the negative log-likelihood. Averaged over a dataset, minimizing it is maximum likelihood: pick the parameters that make the training data as probable as possible. (Using KL instead changes nothing, since H(p) is a constant.)

The model produces scores (logits) z, and the softmax turns them into probabilities, qk = ezk/Σj ezj. Think of it as "market shares": big scores take a disproportionate share. The gradient of the loss with respect to a logit is

∂L / ∂zk = qk − pkbelief minus truth
In plain English

A good coach says how wrong and in which direction: "you were 66% sure it was a cat and it was, so push up by 34 points; you gave dog 24%, push that down 24." The gradient is exactly that.

Worked example. Logits z = (2.0, 1.0, 0.1) give ez = (7.389, 2.718, 1.105), sum 11.212, so q = (0.659, 0.242, 0.099):

True classq for the truthLoss = −ln qGradient q − p
cat0.6590.417(−0.341, +0.242, +0.099)
dog0.2421.417(+0.659, −0.758, +0.099)
bird0.0992.317(+0.659, +0.242, −0.901)

The more wrong the prediction, the larger the loss. With the soft target (0.7, 0.2, 0.1) the loss is 0.807 nats, of which H(p) = 0.802 is irreducible and only KL = 0.005 is fixable.

Derivation of ∂L/∂z = q − p, and how to read the loss number

Derivation. L = −Σ pk ln qk = −Σ pk zk + ln Σj ezj (using Σ pk = 1). Differentiating with respect to zm gives −pm + ezm/Σj ezj = qm − pm. The log-sum-exp term is convex, so the loss is convex in the logits.

Perplexity. exp(average loss) is the effective number of choices the model feels it is making. An average of 3.0 nats per token gives e3.0 = 20.1, as unsure as choosing uniformly among about 20 tokens (4.33 bits per token). Lower is better.

Sequences. A sentence's log-probability is the sum of its token log-probabilities, so language-model training is this loss at every position.

Accuracy. In bits, the loss upper-bounds the error: a wrong top-1 prediction means q for the truth is at most 0.5, so the loss is at least 1 bit. But cross-entropy also rewards how much probability you gave the truth, so it keeps improving after accuracy plateaus.

Regression is cross-entropy too. Squared error is the negative log-likelihood under Gaussian noise (−ln N(y; μ, σ²) = (y − μ)²/2σ² + const); absolute error is the same under Laplace noise. Choosing a loss is choosing the noise you believe in.

5. The family of losses derived from cross-entropy

The pattern is "average information under a target, measured with the model's beliefs". These are the ones you will meet most:

LossFormulaPlain-English idea, and where you meet it
Negative log-likelihood, categorical cross-entropy−Σk pk log qkThe quiz-show penalty. How improbable the model thought what really happened was.
Binary cross-entropy−[ y log ŷ + (1−y) log(1−ŷ) ]The yes/no version; also multi-label tasks, one yes/no question per label.
KL divergenceΣ p log(p/q) = H(p, q) − H(p)Only the extra uncertainty from using q instead of p. Soft targets, distillation, VAEs.
Label smoothingH( (1−ε)·onehot + ε/K, q )A dose of humility: never be 100% sure.
Focal loss−(1 − pt)γ log ptStop grading easy homework; focus on hard questions. Object detection.
DistillationT² · KL( softmax(zt/T) ‖ softmax(zs/T) )Learn from the teacher's hesitation, not just its final answer.
InfoNCE (contrastive)−log [ es(x,x⁺) / Σj es(x,xj) ]Pick the true partner out of a lineup. CLIP, SimCLR.

KL is asymmetric, and that changes what you learn

In plain English

One guard watches a building with two doors. Forward KL(p ‖ q) fires the guard if any door goes unwatched, so the guard stands in the middle and covers both (blurrily). Reverse KL(q ‖ p) fires the guard for standing where there is no door, so the guard picks one door and watches it precisely. Cross-entropy training is the forward kind; variational inference is the reverse kind.

Others you might be missing: the wider f-divergence family (Rényi, Hellinger, total variation, χ²); the Brier score (squared error on probabilities, also a proper scoring rule but gentler on confident mistakes); CTC (negative log-likelihood summed over alignments); ordinal and hierarchical losses; Wasserstein distance (not derived from cross-entropy; it respects distances between outcomes); and the cross-entropy method, a sampling-based optimizer that repeatedly fits a distribution to the best samples.

Binary cross-entropy by hand

Labels y = (1, 0, 1, 1), predictions ŷ = (0.9, 0.2, 0.6, 0.1) give losses −ln 0.9 = 0.105, −ln 0.8 = 0.223, −ln 0.6 = 0.511, −ln 0.1 = 2.303, for a mean of 0.785. The one confident mistake costs almost three times as much as the other three together.

Label smoothing, temperature, and distillation

Label smoothing (ε = 0.1, K = 3) turns the "cat" target into (0.933, 0.033, 0.033); the loss for our logits becomes 0.514 instead of 0.417. A temperature T flattens the softmax: our output (0.659, 0.242, 0.099) at T = 1 becomes (0.445, 0.319, 0.236) at T = 3, which exposes how the teacher sees the wrong classes ("dark knowledge"). Gradients shrink by 1/T², so the distillation loss is multiplied by T².

Focal loss

Thousands of easy examples can drown the few hard ones. Focal loss multiplies the cross-entropy by (1 − pt)γ. At γ = 2, a correct prediction at pt = 0.9 has its loss cut 100× (0.105 → 0.001), while one at pt = 0.1 is cut only 19% (2.30 → 1.87).

6. Why cross-entropy is such a good loss

  1. It is maximum likelihood: the natural way to ask a model for a probability, and statistically efficient.
  2. It is a strictly proper scoring rule: the expected loss is lowest only when you report the true probabilities, so it rewards honesty (a forecaster paid for calibration).
  3. Its gradient does not die: q − p stays large when the model is confidently wrong.
  4. It is convex in the logits, decomposes over examples and tokens, and has units (bits, perplexity) you can interpret.
In plain English

Cross-entropy is a smoke alarm that gets louder the worse the fire. Squared error on a sigmoid goes quiet when the room is thickest with smoke, because its gradient is multiplied by something that shrinks to zero when the unit is saturated. Figure 8 shows it: a unit that starts confidently wrong (z = −5) gets a gradient of ≈ −1 from cross-entropy but only −0.013 from squared error, 75× weaker.

Where it can bite
  • Over-confidence (it keeps rewarding probabilities closer to 1): use label smoothing or temperature scaling.
  • Noisy labels and imbalance: a wrong label with a confident prediction costs a lot, and majority classes dominate the sum: use robust losses, class weights, or focal loss.
  • No notion of distance: confusing "cat" with "dog" costs the same as "cat" with "truck".

7. In practice

PyTorch snippet, a short checklist, and further reading

The worked example from Section 4, by hand and with the library. Both give 0.4170.

import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

z = torch.tensor([[2.0, 1.0, 0.1]])    # logits: shape (batch, classes)
y = torch.tensor([0])                  # the true class index (cat)

q = F.softmax(z, dim=1)                # [[0.6590, 0.2424, 0.0986]]
manual = -torch.log(q[0, y])           # 0.4170, the textbook formula
library = F.cross_entropy(z, y)        # 0.4170, from logits directly

# Binary / multi-label: pass logits, not probabilities
loss = F.binary_cross_entropy_with_logits(logit, target)
  • Pass logits, not probabilities. F.cross_entropy applies the log-softmax itself; applying softmax first is a classic silent bug. Working from logits also uses the log-sum-exp trick, so there is never a log(0).
  • Pick the output layer to match the task. One true class: softmax with categorical cross-entropy. Several labels may be true: one sigmoid per label with binary cross-entropy.
  • Imbalance: class weights, then focal loss. Over-confidence: label smoothing (ε about 0.05 to 0.1) or temperature scaling on a validation set.
  • Sanity check: a starting loss near ln K means the model is guessing uniformly (ln 10 = 2.303 for 10 classes). A validation loss that rises while accuracy holds usually means growing over-confidence.
  • Units: frameworks report nats; divide by ln 2 for bits, or exponentiate for perplexity.

Takeaways

  • Entropy is average uncertainty (the log of the effective number of outcomes); cross-entropy is the average uncertainty under a wrong model; KL is the extra.
  • Training with cross-entropy is maximum likelihood, with the clean gradient q − p, and the same quantities are Gibbs entropy and free energy in physics.
  • Most probabilistic losses are cross-entropy or KL with a different target, direction, or weight.
